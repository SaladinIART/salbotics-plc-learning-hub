The one-sentence version

A PLC (Programmable Logic Controller) is a tough little computer. It watches switches and sensors and follows a written set of rules. Then it turns motors, lamps and valves on or off. It repeats this many times a second. It is built to run for years in dusty, hot, noisy factories.

You already meet PLC-style logic every day: lifts, escalators, automatic gates, washing machines and traffic lights. This page uses those to explain the words you will meet in the rest of the hub.

Read this first

The examples are simplified to teach the idea. Real lifts, gates and escalators use certified safety circuits and follow national rules, and their exact behaviour differs by maker. This hub is a simulated teaching tool, not certified control software.

1. The 3-step loop every PLC repeats

read, decide, act

Step 1 - Read the inputs

Everyday idea: you look around before you cross the road. The PLC checks every button, switch and sensor. It writes down what each one says right now: pressed or not, door closed or open, water high or low.

Technical term: input scan.

Step 2 - Decide using the rules

Everyday idea: a checklist. "If the lid is closed and Start is pressed and there is no emergency, then run". The PLC goes through its rules from top to bottom. It uses only the snapshot it just took.

Technical term: program execution (logic solve).

Step 3 - Act on the outputs

Everyday idea: you take the step. The PLC switches the motor, lamp or valve to match the decision. Then it starts again at step 1.

Technical term: output update. One full lap is a scan cycle. In this trainer a scan is 0.1 second.

Try it: you tap a button for only 0.01 s but the PLC looks at its inputs once every 0.1 s. What might happen?

It can miss the tap completely. The PLC only looks once per lap, and the press was over before the next look. That is why real systems use sensors and wiring that hold the signal long enough, or a latch that remembers the press.

Technical term: scan time / input sampling; latch (seal-in)

Why does this matter? Because the PLC only "knows" what it saw at the start of the lap. A press shorter than one lap can be missed. That is why real systems choose sensors and timing carefully.

2. Inputs and outputs in everyday machines

what the PLC listens to, and what it controls

Inputs (the "senses")

  • Push buttons - the floor button in a lift, Start on a washer.
  • Limit switches - "the gate has reached its end".
  • Photo-eyes - the invisible beam that detects a person or car.
  • Level / pressure sensors - "enough water in the drum".

Outputs (the "muscles")

  • Motors - lift cable drive, escalator steps, gate opener, drum.
  • Valves / pumps - water in and drain in a washer.
  • Lamps / buzzers - floor indicator, "door closing" chime.
  • Brakes / door locks - hold something still, keep a lid shut.

On / off versus "how much"

Many signals are simply on or off (digital): a button is pressed or not. Others vary smoothly (analog): temperature, water level, speed. Start with on/off - it is enough to understand almost every example below.

3. Washing machine: a sequence with timers

one step after another, each waiting for a condition
  1. Check the lid - lid sensor says closed? If not, do nothing.
  2. Fill - open the water valve until the level sensor says "full".
  3. Wash - run the drum back and forth for a set time.
  4. Drain - run the pump until the level sensor says "empty".
  5. Spin - spin fast for a set time, then stop and unlock the lid.

Layman point: it is a recipe. The PLC will not move to the next line until the current one is finished.

Technical terms: this is a sequence (a "state machine"): the machine is always in exactly one step. The "wash for 10 minutes" part is a timer. "Do nothing if the lid is open" is an interlock: one condition that must be true before something else is allowed.

Try it: the lid is opened in the middle of the spin. What should the machine do?

Cut power to the drum motor straight away and keep the lid locked until the drum has stopped. The lid sensor is a higher-priority condition than the spin step.

Technical term: interlock; priority of a safety input

4. Lift (elevator): remembering requests and staying safe

latching, interlocks and priorities

What you see

You press the "5" button and it lights up. You let go, but it stays lit until the lift arrives. The doors close first, then the lift moves, then the lamp goes out.

What the PLC is doing

  • Latch: the button press is remembered even after you let go.
  • Interlock: do not move unless the door sensor says "closed".
  • Limit switch: the floor sensor tells it "you are at floor 5, stop".
  • Priority: overload or emergency stop beats every button.

The same pattern in this trainer

The trainer's PED_NS pedestrian request is a latch too: press once and the request is remembered until that direction gets its green. Likewise its EMERGENCY input beats the normal cycle.

Try it: the lift is at floor 1 and two people press floors 3 and 5 within a second. Why doesn't it forget the second press?

Each press is stored (the button lamp stays lit) until that floor has been served. The PLC does not rely on you holding the button down.

Technical term: latch (seal-in) / request memory

5. Automatic gate: inputs from the world, safety first

limit switches, a safety beam and a timer
  1. Remote is pressed (input) - the gate motor starts opening (output).
  2. The "fully open" limit switch fires - the motor stops.
  3. A timer waits, for example 20 seconds, then starts closing.
  4. If the safety beam is blocked while closing, the gate stops and reopens.

Layman point: the gate does not "know" a car is there - it just reacts to a beam being broken. Technical point: the safety beam is a high-priority input that overrides the closing sequence. On real gates this safety function is handled by certified safety devices, not by ordinary program logic alone.

Try it: a cat walks through the safety beam while the gate is closing. What should happen?

The gate stops immediately and reopens. The beam is a high-priority input, so it overrides the closing step and its timer.

Technical term: safety input overriding a sequence; input priority

6. Escalator: run on demand, stop on danger

start conditions, run timer and emergency stop
  1. A sensor at the entrance sees a person (input).
  2. The motor runs the steps (output) and a run-timer starts.
  3. Each new person restarts the timer; with nobody for a while it stops (or slows).
  4. Pressing the red emergency stop cuts the motor and applies the brake at once.

Technical idea: the emergency stop is wired so that "no signal" means "stop" (fail-safe). If a wire breaks, the machine stops instead of carrying on.

Try it: the wire to the red emergency-stop button is cut by accident. Does the escalator keep running?

No. A healthy, unpressed button sends a signal all the time. If that signal disappears, the escalator stops. It does not matter whether the button was pressed or the wire was cut. Failing into the safe state is deliberate.

Technical term: fail-safe wiring (normally-closed contact)

7. Rice cooker: keeping something at the right temperature

on/off control with a safe gap
  1. You press "Cook". The heater switches on (output).
  2. A temperature sensor watches the pot (input).
  3. While water is boiling, the pot stays near 100 °C. When the water is gone, the temperature rises.
  4. The cooker sees that rise, switches the heater off and changes to "Keep warm".
  5. In "Keep warm" the heater clicks on when the rice cools a little, and off when it is warm enough.

Layman point: it is like turning a kettle on and off by hand to keep tea warm, but automatic. Technical idea: the target temperature is the setpoint. Switching fully on or off around it is on/off control. The small gap between "switch on" and "switch off" temperatures is called hysteresis. Many simple cookers do this with a mechanical thermostat. Smarter ones use a small controller doing the same logic.

Try it: the lid is lifted for a second and the temperature dips slightly. Why doesn't the heater flick on and off every second?

Because of the gap. The heater switches on only below a lower limit. It switches off only above an upper limit. Without that gap it would chatter and wear out.

Technical term: hysteresis; setpoint

8. Car-park barrier: never lower on a car

a road sensor, a timer and an interlock
  1. A driver takes a ticket or presses a button (input).
  2. The barrier arm motor raises the arm until the "up" limit switch fires.
  3. A coil buried in the road senses the metal of the car sitting over it.
  4. When the road sensor shows the car has gone, a short timer runs. Then the arm lowers.

Layman point: the barrier does not "see" the car. It only knows whether the road sensor is occupied. Technical idea: the road sensor is an inductive loop. "Never lower while the loop is occupied" is an interlock. The timer only starts after the loop is clear. Real barriers add extra safety devices, such as a safety edge on the arm.

Try it: a second car follows closely behind the first. Why doesn't the arm come down on it?

The road sensor is still occupied, so the interlock blocks lowering. The timer only starts once the sensor is clear, so a close follower keeps the arm up.

Technical term: interlock; inductive loop sensor

10. Now use the trainer

a traffic light is the same ideas in one small package
  1. Press START. It runs one direction green, then yellow, then both red, then the other direction green. That is a sequence with timers.
  2. Turn on a pedestrian request. It is remembered (latch) and served during that direction's green.
  3. Turn on EMERGENCY. Everything goes red at once: the highest-priority input wins.
  4. Turn START and STOP on together. The controller declares a fault and stays all-red until you reset it. That is a safe, deliberate reaction to contradictory inputs.
